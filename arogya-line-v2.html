The problem

The problem isn’t one broken screen.
It’s the handoff.

Healthcare rarely happens in one place. A concern starts at home. An ASHA worker helps assess it. A patient may need to visit a PHC. A doctor adds advice. Then the family needs to understand what happens next. Each step involves a different person, a different context, and often a different way of accessing the system.

How might I keep one patient’s care journey coherent as it moves between the family, the ASHA worker, and the PHC?

Three users. Three contexts. One problem.

The ASHA

A community health worker, on foot, from house to house, often without signal.

  • Who needs me next?
  • What do I need to do?
  • What happens when I’m offline?
A smartphone

The PHC doctor

At the primary health centre, seeing the day’s patients one after another.

  • Who is coming?
  • Why are they here?
  • What happened before they arrived?
A desktop

The family

At home, often sharing one basic phone, caring for someone who is unwell.

  • What should I do now?
  • What did the doctor say?
  • When should I seek help again?
Any phone, even a basic one

The approach

The product isn’t three interfaces.
It’s one care record moving between three people.

All three surfaces read and write the same record. A booking the ASHA saves offline appears on the doctor’s list the moment it syncs, and the advice the doctor saves is what the family hears on the line.

Interrupted connectivity

The ASHA’s work can’t wait for signal, so saving never depends on it.

Different devices

A smartphone for the ASHA, any phone for the family, a desktop at the PHC.

Different mental models

The ASHA thinks in households, the doctor in appointments, the family in one person and tonight.

Safety over automation

The system screens and suggests; a person decides, and a person is always one key away.

Four rules governed every screen.

01

One screen. One decision.

One primary action per screen, at the thumb. The ASHA never has to choose between two things that look equally important.

02

Never make the user remember the system.

What was booked, what was said and what is waiting to send is shown where it matters, not recalled from an earlier screen.

03

Never use colour as the message.

Pink, yellow and green come from the IMNCI chart the ASHA trained on, and always travel with a word or an icon.

04

Every action creates the next action.

Each step hands something to a named person: a booking to the doctor, advice to the family, a follow-up call to the ASHA.

      What it taught me

      This wasn’t a UI problem.
      It was a product-model problem.

      The system once had enough information to book, but not enough information to know who the booking was for. On the voice line, pressing 0 from the menu booked a visit for the family head, a patient nobody had chosen, and “pregnant woman” could start pregnancy questions for a man. The fix wasn’t a better button. The person is now chosen by name, never by kind, the booking is read back before anything is written, and 0 reaches a person without creating a booking at all.

      What the prototype shows

      Designed in code,
      not just on canvas

      I translated the system into a working prototype so the handoffs could be experienced, not just represented in static screens. All three surfaces read and write one shared record in the browser.

      One journey, three interfaces

      A single patient journey can span three interfaces without losing context.

      Offline, made explicit

      Offline states can be made explicit rather than hidden.

      Voice extends access

      Voice can extend access without requiring a smartphone.

      Edge cases are core UX

      Edge cases can be treated as core UX rather than exceptions.

      This is a prototype, not a production healthcare system. Clinical rules are illustrative, and production would require clinical validation, secure identity, real telephony, persistent offline storage, and approved healthcare infrastructure. All names, families and data are invented.

      What’s next

      What I would solve next

      Production

      • Secure identityVerify the caller before anything is read out, on shared phones and found cards.
      • Persistent offline dataLocal storage, a sync queue with retries and conflict rules.
      • Real availabilityClinic capacity, cancellations and rescheduling across all three surfaces.

      Access

      • Multilingual voiceOne real flow in Kannada, end to end on the line, before any other language.
      • AccessibilityQuestions and advice read aloud in the ASHA app.
      • Shared-device scenariosMedical details spoken aloud in a crowded room.

      Clinical

      • Validated protocolsQuestions and thresholds from an approved protocol with an owner and a review date.
      • Clinical governanceA clinical note kept apart from the message the family hears.
      • Escalation policiesWhat happens after “come now”: seen, admitted, referred or sent home.
