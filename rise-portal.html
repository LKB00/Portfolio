Case study · Rise Portal

The payout arrived. The explanation didn’t.

Rise Portal is the first product Rupeezy built for its digital partners. Partners helped bring business to Rupeezy, but they couldn’t see how their referrals and their clients’ activity turned into earnings. I designed a 0 to 1 portal that connects referral progress, client activity and earnings in one place.

Context

Partners started the journey and got paid. Everything in between was out of sight.

A partner shared a referral link. Someone signed up, completed KYC, opened an account and started trading. The partner earned a share of the brokerage that trading produced, paid monthly on the 15th.

The partner could see the first step and the last. The steps in between lived in spreadsheets and with Rupeezy’s teams, so the only way to see them was to call, raise a ticket or ask a relationship manager.

Referring more people did not automatically mean earning more. How many leads converted, how many clients traded, how much they traded and the share for that period could all change. Seeing only the total, a lower payout could look arbitrary.

“Last month I referred 100 people and earned X. This month I referred 200 and earned less. Why?”

A partner, relayed by support. A question the support team heard often, not an interview quote.

Why it mattered

Rupeezy wasn’t hiding anything, but opacity can look like hiding. It also sent the same routine questions back to the support team, and it left the partner team without a product to show prospective partners.

Evidence and constraints

I learned the brokerage model through the signals I could access

This was my first project at Rupeezy, and I had never worked in broking. The PRD used terms I needed to understand before I could design anything, so I learned the business before I opened Figma.

Lead
a referred person who signed up
Client

Active client

Dashboard

Answer “How am I doing?” before anything else

The dashboard had to answer the money question first, then show where progress might have stalled.

1 2 3 4 5
  • 1

    Current, last payout and total earnings. The money question, answered first.

  • 2

    A daily trend for the last 7, 15 or 30 days, so a change has context.

  • 3

    Active and inactive clients, not just a total.

  • 4

    The lead funnel: Account Processing, Opened and Traded. Where progress stalled.

  • 5

    Payout eligibility. Present, but lower, so it doesn’t take over the first look.

The order follows how often each thing is checked. Earnings and clients are looked at every visit. The funnel matters when something looks off. Eligibility matters most while a partner is working towards it.

Clients

From a total to a list you can act on

A count of leads doesn’t tell a partner who to call. My Clients tracked each referral through Account Processing, Account Opened and Account Traded. Opened is not the same as active: an account can open and never trade. So the list was split into active clients, inactive clients and leads, and sorted by last traded date.

1 2 3
  • 1

    The funnel, with Processing, Opened and Traded as separate counts.

  • 2

    Active clients, inactive clients and leads, each a tab of its own.

  • 3

    Last traded, so a client who has gone quiet stands out.

The hope was that partners would use this to decide who to follow up with: a referral stuck in processing, a client who had stopped trading. That was a design hypothesis before launch, not something I tested with partners.

Earnings

My Earnings showed current, pending, last payout and total earnings, plotted against clients, with brokerage and earnings for each client. A Payout History tab sat beside it, with the cycle, amount, deductions, bank details and date.

Brokerage and earnings per client, so a total can be traced back to the people behind it.

Two decisions

Two constraints that shaped the UI

A rule I could not change, made readable

Partners were not paid automatically every cycle. The business set the rule. My job was to show progress towards each condition, and say plainly why a partner wasn’t eligible yet.

10+ active clientsAlways requiredExample: 7 / 10
AND
30%+ of clients activeEither thisExample: 25% / 30%
OR
5+ new clients this cycleOr thisExample: 3 / 5

Not eligible yet: the first condition isn’t met, and neither of the other two is.

The shipped card shows each condition as progress. It reads And, then Or, from left to right, but it doesn’t show the grouping. The rule above spells that out.

“A message saying only ‘you’re not eligible for payout’ would produce exactly the phone call the whole product was built to remove.”

Kunal, Product Manager

The call
Where should payout eligibility appear on the dashboard?
Considered
Place it at the top, because payout eligibility determines whether a partner receives a payout.
Chose
Place it lower on the page, bottom right. Earnings and client activity are checked repeatedly; eligibility matters most while a partner is working toward the threshold.
Cost
Partners unfamiliar with the rule may need to scroll to find it.

I also considered hiding the card once a partner qualified, and rejected it. A dashboard that rearranges itself by state is harder to learn than one that stays still.

Phone numbers stay masked

The portal could not show full client phone numbers. Each row showed a client’s name, a masked number, their status and last activity: enough to match the record with details a partner already held.

XXXXXX7890 is enough to find a record you already hold, and nothing on its own.

The call
How much client detail can a partner see?
Constraint
Full phone numbers could not be shown.
Chose
Name, masked number, status and last activity. Enough to identify the record, while limiting exposure.
Cost
Partners who hadn’t kept their own referral records could spot a stalled lead and still not have enough to contact the person. I didn’t solve that.

These are outcomes reported by the teams closest to the data, not design impact I measured. No baseline was agreed at the start, I didn’t own analytics, and I can’t say the portal alone caused them.

What I’d test next

The gap I’d close first

The biggest gap was that no partner saw the design before launch, and nobody agreed how we’d measure success. Here is how I’d close it now. None of this was done.

Recommended

  1. Talk to three to five partners as a first round. Ask them to explain an earnings change using the portal, and show where a referral stalled.
  2. Task-based tests: understand a payout, tell opened from active, work out what eligibility still needs, decide who to follow up with.
  3. Agree an analytics plan with the business and support teams before launch.

Measures I’d track

Lead-to-client conversion. Repeat support contacts about payouts and conversion. Whether partners can explain the payout factors in a task. Time and errors when finding a stalled referral. Use of the funnel and client views. No targets until the team agrees them.

A dashboard isn’t the same as solving a visibility problem. Partners need enough of the trail to see what happened, why earnings changed, and what they can do next.
