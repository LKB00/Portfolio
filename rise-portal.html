The short version

Around 100 digital partners drove 35% of Rupeezy’s revenue. The entire relationship ran on spreadsheets and phone calls.

A partner could refer more people one month and earn less, with no way to find out why. Their income was calculated somewhere they couldn’t see.

I designed the portal that made the funnel and the payout calculation visible. Lead-to-client conversion rose 2.4×, and partner support tickets fell 48%.

01 — The problem

The phone call that happened a hundred times a day

Rupeezy’s digital partners are experienced traders and financial content creators — people with an audience that trusts them on money. Around 100 of them drove roughly 35% of company revenue, and the entire relationship ran on spreadsheets and phone calls.

How the model works

01

Partner shares their referral link

02

Someone enters their mobile number — a lead

03

The lead completes KYC and becomes a client

04

The client trades; the partner earns a share of the brokerage — typically around 40%.

The share varied by partner. Payouts landed on the 15th of every month.

A partner wanting to know how many leads had converted called their relationship manager, who opened a spreadsheet and read the numbers back. A hundred partners, referring people daily, each entitled to ask at any time.

There’s a legitimate answer — earnings depend on how many leads completed KYC, how many clients traded, and how much. The partner can’t see any of it. All they can see is that they did more and got less.

Not a reporting gap

When a third of your revenue flows through people who can’t verify their own income, you don’t have a reporting gap.

You have a trust problem.

02 — Groundwork

Learning a business I’d never seen

This was my first project at Rupeezy. The PRD landed full of words I didn’t know — digital partners, brokerage share, payout cycles, lead-to-client conversion.

So before designing anything, I learned the machine. I sat with the PM to understand how the partner business worked and where the money moved, and with the support team to go through what partners actually asked.

The support team had heard the same thing for months.

“I onboard 15–20 users every week, but I have no idea how they’re performing.”

— Mukesh Nangia, Business Development Manager, Rupeezy
Question one“How many of my leads converted?”
Question two“How much will I earn this cycle?”

Every screen I designed answers one of these two.

The honest limitation

I never spoke to a digital partner directly. What I had was the next best thing — the people who answered their calls every day. I come back to this at the end.

03 — The design

Four screens, one job: answer before they ask.

Rise Portal shipped as responsive web and I designed both ends — the same four sections in a bottom navigation on mobile, with charts and tables restacked for one thumb.

3.1

The dashboard

Earnings three ways — current cycle, last payout, all-time — with a daily chart, so a partner sees the shape of it and not just the total. Below that, the funnel: total leads, then the three states a lead can be in.

—The funnel is the answer to “why did I earn less” — partners watch their own referrals move and see where the drop happens.
—The referral link always at hand, with a checkbox: “Do you want to show your name to referred clients?” Transparency runs in both directions.
rise.rupeezy.in

3.2

Earnings

Earnings plotted against total clients and against traded clients, so a partner can see which of their clients actually generate revenue. Below it, full payout history — bank account, cycle, amount, deductions, date.

—Deductions are shown explicitly, as a line item, not netted off silently.
—A payout can be traced back to the clients who generated it.
The whole point

Nothing about the money is computed out of sight.

rise.rupeezy.in

3.3

Clients

Every client with their last-traded date, split into active and inactive.

—Active and inactive as the primary split — not signup date, not alphabet.
—Last-traded date makes “who do I call today” a one-glance decision.

04 — The mechanism

The mechanism nobody was paying for

A partner sees a lead stuck at “account processing.” That lead is money sitting one step away from becoming a client. So the partner calls them — is something wrong with the registration? Can I help? That call used to be Rupeezy’s job. Support teams chase stuck registrations everywhere. But the partner’s call works better, because the lead already knows and trusts the person who referred them.

Same with the inactive clients list. An inactive client earns the partner nothing, so partners nudge them back to trading — because their income depends on it.

Nobody instructed partners to do any of this. The product made their pipeline visible, and self-interest did the rest.

What it actually built

Rise Portal turned 100 partners into a distributed sales and support layer — one that costs nothing, and converts better than a cold call from an unknown number.

I found out about this after launch, from the business team. I designed the visibility. The behaviour was theirs.

The outcome

The biggest outcome wasn’t a screen. It was a behaviour.

05 — Constraint

Designing inside compliance

The mechanism in the last section depends on partners contacting their own referrals. As a regulated broker, we cannot expose client phone numbers — not to partners, not to anyone.

The answer came from how partners actually work. Most collect contact details themselves when they refer someone, through their own forms and conversations. What they need from the portal isn’t the number — it’s the match.

The principle

I didn’t design against the constraint. I designed the smallest sufficient signal around it — a name, the masked digits, a status, so a partner can connect what they see here to the records they already hold.

06 — Eligibility

Making “X AND (Y OR Z)” readable in a glance

6.1

Making the rule readable

Partners don’t automatically get paid every cycle. The business set a compound rule to keep partners driving real trading activity rather than collecting signups.

The rule wasn’t mine. My problem was making it legible — partners needed to read their own status in a glance, not parse a logic expression.

“The eligibility card is the one place in the product that delivers bad news. A message saying only ‘you’re not eligible for payout’ would produce exactly the phone call the whole product was built to remove — a partner reading it would call their relationship manager to ask why, and whether it’s permanent.”

— Kunal, Product Manager, Rise Portal

Each condition became its own fraction, with the AND/OR relationship visible between them, and a plain-language warning when a partner falls short. Someone three clients short can see precisely which lever to pull.

6.2

Where the card sat

Where this card sat caused the one real disagreement of the project.

The business wanted eligibility at the top of the dashboard — it decides whether a partner gets paid, so surely it’s the most important thing on the page.

I argued the opposite. Eligibility is one-time information: a partner needs it while working toward the threshold, and stops looking once they’ve met it. Earnings and clients are what they check every visit. It went bottom-right — visible, not dominant.

I also considered hiding the card once a partner qualified, and rejected it. A dashboard that rearranges itself depending on state is worse than one stable layout.

Design principle

Important and frequent are not the same thing. Layout should follow frequency.

07 — Results

What happened

2.4x

Lead-to-client conversion

In the two months after launch — reported by the business team from the partner dashboard.

48%

Fewer partner support tickets

Earnings and lead-status queries — the two questions that dominated call logs — became self-serve. Reported by the support team and the partner relationship managers.

1st

The portal became a sales asset

When onboarding new digital partners, the business finally had something to show — this is exactly how transparent your earnings will be. To our knowledge, no competitor offered partners anything comparable at the time.

The caveat on these numbers

Rise Portal was isolated to digital partners and the commission structure didn’t change in that window, but I’d want to verify nothing moved on the payout side before claiming clean causality. I didn’t own the analytics — I’m reporting what the teams closest to the data reported.

08 — Honesty

What I’d do differently

Three things were true about this project.

No direct contact

I never spoke to a digital partner. Everything I knew arrived second-hand, through the PM and the support team who answered their calls. Five conversations before designing might have surfaced the follow-up behaviour early enough to build for it, rather than enabling it and finding out afterwards.

No plan for the numbers

Rupeezy didn’t do post-release analysis, and nobody agreed upfront what this product was supposed to move. The 2.4× and the 48% exist because I went and asked the business and support teams myself. A metric you chase down afterwards is a weaker thing than one you committed to in advance.

No evidence, only reasoning

I won the placement argument by making a case about frequency and importance. I was right, and I had nothing to show. Five partners’ behaviour on a screen recording would have settled it in a minute instead of a meeting — and would have told me whether I was right for the reason I thought.

What I took from it

Shipping isn’t the finish line. The first thing I’d change anywhere I work next is agreeing what a feature should move before design starts — so there’s something to learn from afterwards.

That’s Rise Portal, including the parts that didn’t go well.
