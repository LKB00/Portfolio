Designing an AI agent that fights for your refund

How I designed an AI agent people can trust with their money and their name: what the AI decides, what code calculates, and what they always approve themselves.

Try the prototype
Type
Self-initiated, not client work. Not shipped to real users.
Timeline
Sep 2026 · about 2 weeks, full-time
Role
Solo: research, strategy, design, prompts and eval design.
For
Young Indians, 20–30, with ₹500–₹20,000 stuck with a company
Status
Live prototype. Gemini reads what people send; sending is simulated. The evaluation (eval) and user testing haven’t been run yet.

The short version

The problem

People like Ananya, 24, have a right to their refund and a free government channel that works. They don’t know the rule or the deadline, and run out of time to chase. So the company keeps the money.

The insight

Many tools, including ChatGPT, can draft a complaint. None of them run the whole fight: track the deadline, follow up, escalate. And the worst moment isn’t losing the money. It’s having no next step.

What’s proven, and what isn’t

Proven: a live prototype anyone can use, and five errors that would have cost people money, found and fixed before anyone used it. Not yet: the evaluation and testing with real people. Both results will go here, failures included.

How I worked

I designed in code, with a real AI model, not in static mock-ups. The product screens on this page are the working prototype itself, and Gemini reads what people send, so I designed against its actual answers and mistakes, not imagined ones. That’s how I found it inventing years in dates (chapter 2). An AI coding assistant wrote much of the code; the research, decisions, prompts and evaluation are mine.

The process

A Double Diamond, with a layer most processes don’t havePlus prompts, evals and uncertain states. And one rule: every decision needs a reason.

The space, money people lose to companies, was clear early. The right moment to step in wasn’t, and the first diamond earns its time finding it. But a normal Double Diamond has no step for prompts, eval sets or states where the system is unsure. For an AI agent, that’s the design work.

One rule for the whole project

Every decision needed a reason, and the reason had to be a number, a source, a constraint or a principle. “It felt right” didn’t count.

01 · The problem

Choose a problem where AI is the product, not a feature

Which problem is worth an AI agent, who has it, and when is the moment to step in?

The starting question

I didn’t start with refunds. I started with a question.

What could a young Indian not do before LLMs, that they can do now?

Three rounds of ideas

The first two rounds failed my own filter

Round 1

Annoyances, not problems

Failed the filter

Not “what’s painful”, but “what was impossible before LLMs”: an advocate for people who can’t afford one, who knows the rules and never tires of following up.

Research

I couldn’t recruit in time, so I changed the method, not the questions

I planned 5–8 interviews with people holding a stuck refund. Recruiting didn’t come through in time, so I answered the same questions from public evidence: Reddit and X complaints, app reviews, consumer forums, news and government data. It’s labelled desk research everywhere, never primary research.

Who it’s for

Ananya, 24, and the moment she has no next step

Ananya, 24

Bengaluru · proto-persona from desk research

  • First or second job. Pays with UPI, India’s instant phone-to-bank payments, not a card.

  • Orders on Flipkart, Meesho or Myntra about once a month.

The lowest point isn’t the loss. It’s stage 5, when there’s no next step. That’s the moment it’s built for.

The focus moment

She’s just been told “5–7 working days”, again

She’s angry, on her phone, deciding whether to chase again or give up. It’s in almost every source, and it’s when an advocate is worth most. Earlier, she still trusts the company. Later, she’s stopped caring.

Problem statement

She has the right to a refund and a free channel that works, but doesn’t know the rule, the channel or the deadline, and can’t afford the time to keep chasing. So the money stays with the company.

It puts the cause on missing knowledge and missing time, not on her. An AI agent can supply both.

ChatGPT is the real default

People already paste “write a strong complaint” into it. But the letter is 10% of the job. Knowing the rule, where to send it, when to escalate, and following up is the other 90%.

DoNotPay is the cautionary tale

It called itself a “robot lawyer” and paid $193,000 to settle an FTC case for overpromising. Getbaq never claims to be a lawyer, on any screen.

Companies already have AI

Tools like Intercom Fin auto-resolve support tickets. The consumer side has nothing.

The risk, stated openly

The helpline could add its own AI; Voxya could add an LLM. The defence is speed, mobile-first design and respect for her time, not technology.

Three assumptions I changed1915 moved from last resort to first escalation, and two more.

People give up after 2–3 weeks

No give-up trigger

No source supports a fixed timeline. So the product removes follow-up effort from day one.

1915 is a last resort

1915 moved to the front

It’s fast, and 1,000+ companies already respond to it. It became the early, primary escalation.

I know the trust limits

Designed conservatively

There’s no India evidence on fees or trust. So: free to start, approval on every action, and both stated as open risks.

The agent had to know the rules, so I read them first, from the primary sources rather than summaries: the E-Commerce Rules 2020; the circular from RBI, India’s central bank, on failed payments; the RBI Ombudsman, its free complaint service; the refund rules of DGCA, the aviation regulator; and the helpline’s programme that connects it to companies. And I collected 30 real cases, anonymised and sourced, that later became the eval set: 12 e-commerce, 7 travel, 4 UPI, 4 ed-tech or subscription, and 3 that are out of scope or fit no category. Six are traps with no matching rule.

What I saw, and what it meant for the designSeven findings, each turned into a design rule.

What I saw

So the design…

  • Bots repeat “5–7 days” and close tickets

    …makes persistent, dated follow-up the core, not one-time drafting

  • 1915 works, but few know it

    …makes 1915 the next step, prepared for her, and explains it simply

  • ChatGPT drafts but forgets deadlines

    …owns the timeline and the escalation ladder

  • People fear sharing OTP and bank details

    …never asks for them, and strips them if pasted

  • DoNotPay was fined for “robot lawyer” claims

    …says “not legal advice” on every action screen

  • Each rule has a different clock

    …classifies the case and cites the exact rule and deadline

  • People want to know where their case stands

    …keeps the status always visible

Five questions the design had to answerA bot reply to a rule-backed complaint in under a minute, and four more.

Turn a screenshot of a bot reply into a rule-backed complaint in under a minute?

Starting must cost less than one more follow-up.

Let her approve every action in one tap, without reading a legal letter?

Approval is mandatory; reading effort isn’t.

Show what happens next and when, so she stops checking?

The cost is uncertainty, not just money.

Make the agent’s confidence visible, so a wrong rule never surprises her?

Wrong answers are the core AI risk.

Make 1915 feel like a normal step, not scary government stuff?

Awareness is low, and it’s the strongest channel.

Five principles, used to settle every argumentEffort down. Nothing leaves without a tap. Show the doubt.
  • 1

    Effort down, not process up. If a screen adds work, cut it.

  • 2

    Nothing leaves without a tap. Every send is previewed and approved.

  • 3

    Show the rule, the deadline and the doubt. Cite sources. Say when unsure.

  • 4

    Factual, never angry. Short, calm complaints get faster replies.

  • 5

    Honest about limits. It helps you complain. It is not a lawyer.

02 · Where AI stops

The model reads and writes. Code counts.

Which parts need AI judgment, which need exact calculation, and how would I know the AI is right?

The capability map

AI for judgment, code for anything exact, and a list it never touches

Deadlines and money must be exact, so they’re tested code. AI is only needed for judgment on messy text. The “never” column came from the trust research.

And she can see the split: every step is labelled with who did it, and the deadline says Calculated, not AI. Every rule card cites the law it rests on, the way Perplexity puts its sources first, so she can check it instead of taking it on trust.

The legal line

Only enrolled advocates can give legal advice in India. So Getbaq helps her complain and escalate, never advises, and every action screen says so.

Guardrails

The model’s output is never trusted directly

If the model…

Code…

  • Puts an amount or date in the letter she didn’t confirm

    Drops the letter and uses the template, and says so

  • Cites a rule that isn’t on the verified sheet

    Routes to Not sure. It can never reach Approve.

  • Writes more than 150 words

    Asks once to shorten, then drops the draft

  • Says “sue”, “legal action”, “lawyer”, threatens, or uses “!”

    Asks once to rewrite, then drops it

  • Is unsure

    Never shows the Approve button

  • Receives an OTP-like code

    Strips it before the model sees it, and tells her

  • Tries to send without a tap

    Can’t: only a real tap records a send

Bugs I caught

Five mistakes that would have cost people money, caught at the source, and one known gap

01

The UPI deadline was wrong

I gave every failed payment 5 days. RBI gives UPI one working day (T+1); 5 days is for cards. UPI users would have waited 4 days they didn’t have.

02

Two airline clocks were one rule

Card refunds: 7 days. Travel-site bookings: 14 working days. Those would have been flagged late a week early.

03

Deadlines moved with the timezone

Dates were read in the wrong timezone, so near midnight a deadline could wrongly show as missed. All dates now use one fixed timezone.

04

The model invented years

Asked to copy “2 Aug”, Gemini added the years 2023 and 2021. Now a year is kept only if she wrote one.

05

The UPI letter went to the wrong party

It was addressed to the shop, but the RBI rule applies to her bank. Now the app asks for her bank, and the letter goes there.

06

A known gap, documented

“14 working days” is counted as 14 calendar days for now, and the rule sheet says so.

What’s tested today

The code is tested. The model isn’t scored yet.

Everything that has one right answer is tested: 182 automated tests cover dates, money, states and safety, the parts where being wrong costs real money. And I used the prototype myself, which is where the biggest change, pages to one thread, came from.

What isn’t scored yet is the model’s judgment: whether it reads a case correctly and picks the right rule. The rest of this chapter is how that will be measured.

Where this runs today

The full prompt and its guardrails are built and covered by tests, and the live prototype uses them: Gemini reads what she sends, picks the rule, writes the letter and reads the company’s reply. Every answer passes code’s checks first. When one doesn’t, or the free limit runs out, a fixed template does that step instead, and the steps she sees say so (see the audit, chapter 4).

03 · Her control

She approves everything, in her own name

How much should an agent do on its own when it’s her money and her name?

The lifecycle

It proposes, she approves, it acts, it reports back

Every action has the same shape. Escalation isn’t exempt: even after a company has ignored her for weeks, the 1915 complaint still needs her tap.

It says back what it understood before it acts. Before any rule is found or letter written, she sees “You confirmed · Flipkart · ₹3,499 · 2 Aug” and can fix a wrong fact there, not in a finished letter. It’s the pattern known as prompt reflection: the AI restates the request before doing anything with it.

Agentic decisions

No autonomy dial, on purpose

Most agents hand over more control as trust grows. Here, a wrong or rude message damages her, not the AI, so trust never removes the need to approve.

  • Every send needs approval, forever

    The agent acts in her name (the Intent Preview pattern)

  • Escalate through the free helpline

    Official, and big companies already answer it

  • Design for three failures: wrong, ignored, blocked

    Each one needs its own state, not an error message

Approve, up close

Eight decisions on one card

  • 1

    It looks like an email. Familiar on purpose, so the novelty goes on the agent, not the letter. From shows her own name.

  • 2

    The letter is visible. Nobody should send what they haven’t seen.

  • 3

    The amount and deadline come first. The rule and how sure it is sit below, as backing.

  • 4

    One strong button. The only irreversible action looks unlike anything else.

  • 5

    Edit and Not now, equally quiet. Saying no is as easy as editing.

  • 6

    Signed by her, never by Getbaq. Send waits for her name.

  • 7

    Reassurance right where she taps. “6 seconds to undo” sits just above the button.

  • 8

    When it’s unsure, this card never appears. She never approves what the AI doubts.

Every letter ended “Sent via Getbaq”: no name, nothing to match to her order, so nothing a grievance officer could act on. I’d treated “no accounts” as a feature. But a product that complains in someone’s name has to know who she is.

04 · Being wrong

Design for being wrong: the AI’s, and my own

What does she see when it’s unsure, ignored or blocked, and what did I get wrong?

States

Five ways it can go wrong got the same care as the happy path

Failure states are where trust is won or lost. Pick any state to see the real screen, and try them: they all work.

Showing the work

Showing the work, honestly

A blank wait feels like nothing is happening, so the agent narrates its real work. Every step says who did it, so she can tell a judgment from a calculation, and finished steps fold, so the trace never buries the answer.

The rule

Every step shown is real work that just finished. A fake step is the same violation as a fake rule.

A bug I found by using it

Only what it actually read

I sent it a stock chart. It came back as a Flipkart case: ₹3,499, a rusted jar. The demo had been handing every image the sample case’s facts: the same trust failure as an invented rule.

Now

Why

  • Screenshots and typed words are read by Gemini, in one call

    Facts come only from what she sent

  • A screenshot that isn’t about money doesn’t start a case

    “I read that screenshot, but I don’t see an order, a payment or a refund in it.”

  • If the AI can’t be reached, a simpler backup reads it, and says so

    “Read your message with a simpler reader. Check what I found.”

05 · The craft

Smaller decisions that make it feel like one product

The two changes that mattered most, then everything else in a line each.

Iteration

From pages to one thread

Using my own prototype, every step felt like being sent to a new page. I kept the logic the same and changed only how it looks: each step adds to one conversation, and finished steps fold into one-line receipts. It reads like a conversation but isn’t only chat: the steps are cards she can act on. As Perplexity’s head of design puts it, not everything should be a chat.

This did more for it feeling like an agent than any colour, font or animation.

Iteration

One box that takes anything

People won’t sort a bot reply from an order page from a bank SMS. The first two versions made her sort; the third made it the agent’s job.

One box, but not one process. Text is scrubbed for OTPs, then read. A screenshot is read with vision, which is slower, and the trace says so.

Everything else

“It doesn’t look like an AI product”It matched a list of AI design tells. Naming the cause fixed it.

Every fact, rule and confidence block was the same rounded grey card, with a default blue button and a status line joined with middle dots. It matched a known list of AI-generated design tells almost line for line. Once it had a name, I could fix the cause instead of changing colours at random.

Read once, ask onceEvery missing fact asked for in one card.

Missing info used to ask one question per turn: company, then date, each a full round trip for something the agent knew it needed from the start. Now code decides what the case type requires and asks for every gap in one card. Each question says why it’s needed and where to find it, offers likely answers, and moves on by itself.

Two places, one thread per case, and three loopsThree loops, run by code: escalation, correction and missing information.

The loops are the point. They’re what makes this an agent that repeats and escalates, not a form that ends. The escalation ladder itself is decided by code, not the model: company, then the National Consumer Helpline, then e-Jagriti, the government’s site for filing a consumer court case. For UPI: the bank, then the RBI Ombudsman after 30 days.

A bug the motion found

The first version of the phone sheet made the conversation behind it jump 239 pixels as it opened. Giving the sheet keyboard focus while it was still below the screen made the browser scroll to show it. The fix was one option (focus without scrolling), plus a container that can’t scroll at all, so it can’t come back.

Looking back

Reflection

What I’d do differently, and what I’d keep

Differently

  • Run the eval earlier. I polished screens before seeing real AI output. The evaluation should come before the screens.

  • Recruit before designing. Trust limits and willingness to pay are still open. Only real people can close them.

  • Write the marketing from the code. The landing page promised more than the prototype did. The audit caught it; it shouldn’t have had to.

  • Question “no accounts” earlier. A complaint needs a name on it (chapter 3).

Keep

  • The model reads and writes. Code counts.

  • Failure states with the same weight as the happy path.

  • Using the product myself. The biggest fix, one thread, came from how it felt.

How I’ll know it works

One number matters to her. Four more tell me if it’s working.

Money recovered

The only number she cares about. Target: 3 of the first 5 real cases refunded.

Days to refund

Target: inside the rule’s own deadline, beating the manual journey.

Drafts sent unedited

Draft quality, and trust. Target: most sent with no edits, or small ones.

Wrong-rule rate

Measures invented law directly. Target: under 5% on the eval, and zero invented rules.

Follow-ups by the agent

Effort removed. Target: she never writes a follow-up herself.

The targets are hypotheses to test, not results. The riskiest assumptions, ranked: people let an agent send in their name; companies answer rule-backed complaints faster; the helpline accepts agent-prepared ones; the AI picks the right rule; and people will pay, which this project didn’t test.

What people ask firstLegal advice, ChatGPT, screenshots, and five more.
Is this legal advice?

No. It helps you complain and escalate, and never claims to be a lawyer.

Why not just use ChatGPT?

ChatGPT drafts. It doesn’t know your deadline, where to escalate, or follow up when the company goes quiet. Getbaq does all three, and still asks before sending anything.

What happens to my screenshots?

They’re read to build your case, and not kept: the case only notes that you sent one. It never asks for your OTP, card or bank password, and strips an OTP if you paste one.

Does it ever send without me?

No. Every send needs your tap, and you get six seconds to cancel before it goes. In this prototype, nothing is actually sent.

Why do I sign the letter?

Because the complaint is from you, not from Getbaq. Companies match it to your account by your name and the email or phone you use with them.

What if it doesn’t know?

It says so, shows why, and asks.
