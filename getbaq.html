Case study · AI product design · Getbaq

Designing an AI agent that fights for your refund

When a company keeps your money, a lawyer costs more than the refund. Getbaq finds the rule, drafts the complaint, tracks the deadline and escalates when they go quiet. It never sends a word without your tap.

Try the prototype

For

Process
Double Diamond, plus an AI layer for prompts, evals and uncertainty
Status
Working prototype on a real LLM call. Scored eval pending

The short version

The problem

People have a legal right to their refund and a free government channel that works. They don’t know the rule, the channel or the deadline, and they run out of time to keep chasing. So the money stays with the company.

The insight

Drafting a complaint is crowded; ChatGPT already does it. Nobody runs the fight: follows up, tracks the deadline, escalates. And the worst moment isn’t the loss. It’s when there’s no next step.

What I built

An agent where the model reads and writes, and code counts: deadlines and money are computed and tested, never generated. Every send waits for her tap, with six seconds to undo.

Process

A Double Diamond, with a layer most processes don’t have

The space, money people lose to companies, was clear early. The right moment to step in wasn’t, and the first diamond earns its time finding it. But a normal Double Diamond has no step for prompts, eval sets or states where the system is unsure. For an AI agent, that’s the design work.

One rule for the whole project

Every decision needed a reason, and the reason had to be a number, a source, a constraint or a principle. “It felt right” didn’t count.

01

Discover

Finding a problem where AI is the product, not a feature

Is there a real problem worth an AI agent, and who has it?

The starting question

I didn’t start with refunds. I started with a question.

What could a young Indian not do before LLMs, that they can do now?

The goal wasn’t to add AI to something. It was to find a problem where AI is the whole point. Every idea had to pass six tests:

  • 01

    How many people have it?

    Scale.

  • 02

    Do they lose money, time or a chance?

    Real pain, not annoyance.

  • 03

    Do they already try to solve it?

    Proof the pain is real.

  • 04

    Is AI actually needed?

    Not decoration.

  • 05

    Is there room next to existing players?

    Not a copy.

  • 06

    Does it force hard AI design?

    Wrong answers hurt; actions have consequences.

Three rounds of ideas

The first two rounds failed my own filter

  • Round 1

    Annoyances, not problems

    Scam-checking, a read-before-you-sign tool, a screenshot organiser, a money-leak tracker. Nobody loses real money to a messy camera roll, and a big player could ship most of these as a feature in a week.

    Failed the filter

  • Round 2

    Scale, but not sharp

    Job readiness: huge, crowded, vague. Investment-scam protection: ₹22,495 crore lost to cyber fraud in 2025, but very hard to distribute. Communication skills: a weaker trust story.

    Too broad

  • Round 3

    A better question

    Not “what’s painful”, but “what was impossible before LLMs”. The answer: an advocate for people who can’t afford one. It can know the rules, write in the right form, send it to the right place and follow up without getting tired.

    The direction

Research

I couldn’t recruit in time, so I changed the method, not the questions

The plan was 5–8 interviews with people holding a stuck refund: to see their real screenshots and the exact moment they give up. Recruiting didn’t come through in time. So I answered the same interview questions from public evidence: Reddit and X complaints, Play Store reviews, consumer forums, news reports and government data. It’s labelled desk research everywhere, never primary research.

Assumptions

Three things I believed going in, and what I changed

  • People give up after 2–3 weeks

    No give-up trigger

    No source supports a fixed timeline. So the product removes follow-up effort from day one.

  • 1915 is a last resort

    1915 moved to the front

    It’s fast, and 1,000+ companies already respond to it. It became the early, primary escalation.

  • I know the trust limits

    Designed conservatively

    There’s no India evidence on fees or trust. So: free to start, approval on every action, and both stated as open risks.

The agent had to know the rules, so I read them first, from the primary sources rather than summaries: the E-Commerce Rules 2020, RBI’s failed-transaction circular, the RBI Ombudsman, DGCA’s refund rules and the helpline’s convergence programme. And I collected 30 real cases, anonymised and sourced, that later became the eval set.

02

Define

Deciding what the AI is allowed to do, and what it never does

What exactly are we solving, and where does judgment end and arithmetic begin?

The focus moment

She’s just been told “5–7 working days”, again

She’s angry, on her phone, deciding whether to chase again or give up. This moment appears in almost every source, emotion is at its highest, and it’s exactly when an advocate is worth the most. Earlier, she still trusts the company. Later, she’s stopped caring.

Problem statement

She has the right to a refund and a free channel that works, but doesn’t know the rule, the channel or the deadline, and can’t afford the time to keep chasing. So the money stays with the company.

It puts the cause on missing knowledge and missing time, not on her. An AI agent can supply both.

How might we

Five questions the design had to answer

  • Turn a screenshot of a bot reply into a rule-backed complaint in under a minute?

    Starting must cost less than one more follow-up.

  • Let her approve every action in one tap, without reading a legal letter?

    Approval is mandatory; reading effort isn’t.

  • Show what happens next and when, so she stops checking?

    The cost is uncertainty, not just money.

  • Make the agent’s confidence visible, so a wrong rule never surprises her?

    Wrong answers are the core AI risk.

  • Make 1915 feel like a normal step, not scary government stuff?

    Awareness is low, and it’s the strongest channel.

Principles

Five principles, used to settle every argument

  1. 1Effort down, not process up.If a screen adds work, cut it.
  2. 2Nothing leaves without a tap.Every send is previewed and approved.
  3. 3Show the rule, the deadline and the doubt.Cite sources. Say when unsure.
  4. 4Factual, never angry.Short, calm complaints get faster replies.
  5. 5Honest about limits.It helps you complain. It is not a lawyer.

Where AI stops

The model reads and writes. Code counts.

Deadlines and money must be exactly right, so they’re code with unit tests. Judgment on messy text is the only place AI is truly needed. And the “never” column came straight from the trust research.

The split isn’t only architecture; she can see it. Every step the agent shows is labelled with who did it, and the deadline says Calculated, not AI. That’s the one thing a worried person should be able to check.

The legal line

Only enrolled advocates can give legal advice in India. So the product is “help you complain and escalate”, never legal advice, and every action screen says so. Being useful without crossing that line became a design problem in itself.

Success

One number matters to her. Four more tell me if it’s working.

  • Money recovered

    Per case and in total. The only number she cares about.

  • Days to refund

    From first complaint. Proves the agent beats the manual journey.

  • Drafts sent unedited

    Draft quality, and trust.

  • Wrong-rule rate

    On the eval set. Measures hallucination directly.

  • Follow-ups by the agent

    Versus by her. Effort removed.

The riskiest assumptions, ranked: that people will let an agent send in their name; that companies answer rule-backed complaints faster; that the helpline accepts an agent-prepared complaint; that the AI picks the right rule most of the time; and that people will pay, which this project didn’t test.

Information architecture

Two places, one thread per case, and three loops

The loops are the point. They’re what makes this an agent that repeats and escalates, not a form that ends. The escalation ladder itself is decided by code, not the model: company, then the National Consumer Helpline, then e-Jagriti information. For UPI: the bank, then the RBI Ombudsman after 30 days.

Your cases

Fifteen states behind the scenes. She sees four phrases.

Approve, Missing info, Not sure, Blocked and Reply read mean the same thing to her: I have to act. So they share one label, “Needs your input”, and each row says what kind: “Ready to send”, “Missing a detail”.

Sorted by who has to act, not by date. A stalled case that needs her is the one losing her money, so it goes on top. Grounded in AirHelp’s claims dashboard.

03

Develop

Designing an agent that can be wrong, and says so

How does the agent behave, what happens when it fails, and what did I try and throw away?

The lifecycle

It proposes, she approves, it acts, it reports back

Every action has the same shape. Escalation isn’t exempt: even after a company has ignored her for weeks, the 1915 complaint still needs her tap.

Agentic decisions

No autonomy dial, on purpose

Most agents let you hand over more control as trust grows. With money and her name at stake, trust built over time doesn’t remove the need to approve. A wrong or rude message damages her, not the AI.

  • Every send needs approval, forever

    The agent acts in her name (the Intent Preview pattern)

  • Escalate through the free helpline

    Free, official, and big companies are already connected to it

  • Mobile and chat-first

    WhatsApp complaints to the helpline grew from 3% to 20% in two years

  • Design for three failures: wrong, ignored, blocked

    Each one needs its own state, not an error message

States

Five ways it can go wrong got the same care as the happy path

Failure states are where trust is won or lost. Pick any state to see the real screen, and try them: they all work.

Prompt design

Structured output, and a model that’s never given a number to get wrong

The model returns JSON with fixed fields, so code reads the deadlines and next steps, and every field can be validated. She never reads raw model output.

The model gets each rule’s name and description, but never its day count. It has no number to compute a wrong date from.

Guardrails

The model’s output is never trusted directly

If the model…

Code…

  • Cites a rule that isn’t on the verified sheet

    Forces confidence to low and routes to Not sure. It can never reach Approve.

  • Writes more than 150 words

    Asks once to shorten, then drops the draft

  • Says “sue”, “legal action”, “lawyer”, threatens, or uses “!”

    Asks once to rewrite, then drops it

  • Is unsure

    Never shows the Approve button

  • Receives an OTP-like code

    Strips it before the model sees it, and shows her it was removed

  • Tries to send without a tap

    Can’t: a send is only ever recorded from a real tap event

Architecture

Built so an accidental auto-send is impossible, not just forbidden

  • Legal moves, separate from who made them

    The state machine only says which moves are legal. A separate layer decides whether a tap, the model or code caused it.

  • verified: true on every rule

    An unverified rule can never appear in a draft. This is where hallucination is stopped.

  • All dates and money in one tested file

    The one place where being wrong costs real money.

  • The eval before the screens

    If the model picks the wrong rule 40% of the time, the screens are decoration. The score decides what to design.

  • A real model call, not a Figma mock

    The eval needs real output. WhatsApp is simulated in a web app, because the real API takes weeks to approve.

  • Stop, rather than fake it

    Blocked on API billing, I built the eval code-complete and left it honestly unrun.

Bugs I caught

Four mistakes that would have cost people money, caught before any screen

  • 01

    The UPI deadline was wrong

    My first rule gave every failed payment 5 days. RBI gives UPI failures T+1; 5 days is only for ATM and card. Every UPI user would have been told they had 4 days they didn’t have.

  • 02

    Two airline clocks were one rule

    Direct card refunds: 7 days. Travel-agent bookings: 14 working days. MakeMyTrip bookings would have been flagged late a week early.

  • 03

    Deadlines moved with the timezone

    Dates were read in the server’s local time. At UTC+5:30 that flips “missed” near midnight. Now all date maths runs in UTC.

  • 04

    A known gap, documented

    “14 working days” is approximated as 14 calendar days. There’s no business-day logic yet, and the rule sheet says so.

Iteration 01

One box that takes anything

People won’t sort a bot reply from an order page from a bank SMS. They’ll do whatever is fastest. The first two versions made her do that sorting; the third made it the agent’s job.

One box, but not one process. Text is scrubbed for OTPs, then read. A screenshot is read with vision, which is slower, and the trace says so instead of pretending every step costs the same. Text plus a screenshot are checked against each other.

Iteration 02

“It doesn’t look like an AI product”

Every fact, rule and confidence block was the same rounded grey card, with a default blue button and a status line joined with middle dots. It matched a known list of AI-generated design tells almost line for line. Once it had a name, I could fix the cause instead of changing colours at random.

Iteration 03

From pages to one thread

Using my own prototype, every step felt like being redirected to a new page. That’s not how talking to something feels. I kept the state machine exactly the same and changed only the rendering: each step adds to one continuous thread. Later, when threads got long, finished steps started folding into one-line receipts.

This one structural change did more for it feeling like an agent than any colour, font or animation.

Iteration 04

Showing the work, honestly

A blank wait feels like nothing is happening, so the agent narrates its real work. Two later changes made that trustworthy rather than busy: every step says who did it, so she can tell a judgment from a calculation, and finished steps stack and then fold, so the trace never buries the answer.

The rule

Every step shown matches real work that just finished. No fake delays. A fake step is the same trust violation as a fake rule.

Iteration 05

Room for more than one case

The product assumed one case at a time; a returning user with three refunds had nowhere to land. The first fix, a Home screen, didn’t last. Chat products open on a fresh conversation and keep the past one gesture away, so a new case became the opening screen and the list moved into a drawer on a phone and a rail on desktop. On a wide screen, a case file on the right keeps the deadline, the facts and the rule from scrolling away.

Iteration 06

Read once, ask once

Missing info used to ask one question per turn: company, then date, each a full round trip for something the agent knew it needed from the start. Now code decides what the case type requires and asks for every gap in one card. Each question says why it’s needed and where to find it, offers likely answers, and moves on by itself.

Iteration 07

Checked against four frameworks

Google PAIR, Microsoft’s 18 guidelines for human-AI interaction, Shape of AI and Smashing Magazine’s six agentic patterns. Four gaps turned up, and all four are fixed:

The gap

The fix

  • Approval before sending, but no undo after

    A visible six-second countdown after Send it

  • Drafts weren’t labelled as AI-written

    “AI-drafted, based on what you shared” above every letter

  • A case only closed if the AI read a reply

    “I got it resolved another way” on Waiting

  • Animations might ignore accessibility settings

    Every trace and pulse respects reduced motion

Name and mark

A name people say out loud, and a mark that shows money coming back

Every candidate was checked against existing products. Haq was too close to Haqdarshak and a 2025 film. Vasooli means forceful collection. Adhikar is used by many NGOs, Backpay by an existing refund app, Refyn by six others. Getbaq says what it does: get back. Known risk: GetBaqala, a grocery app in the Gulf, flagged for a formal check.

The first icon was a ₹, which put Getbaq beside payments apps and implied it holds people’s money. It never does. The mark had to show the promise instead, which is return.

05

Test

An agent that cites the wrong law is worse than one that admits it doesn’t know

Is the AI honest and correct, and how would I know?

The evaluation

Built to catch the model inventing things

8
rules, each read from the actual legal text and marked verified
30
real, sourced cases, rewritten with testable dates
7
traps where the right answer is “no rule covers this”
3×
runs per case; it passes only if all three pass

Some cases have two acceptable rules, so the model isn’t failed for a fair choice. Several are missing a company, a date or an amount, to test whether it asks instead of guessing. Case type, rule and amount are scored automatically; tone is automatic, then checked by hand; and a person reads every draft for invented facts. An invented rule is an automatic fail, as serious as a wrong deadline.

Error analysis

Fix the source before the prompt, and the prompt before the UI

  1. Collect real outputs
  2. Mark failures by hand
  3. Group them
  4. Fix: rules → prompt → UI

Most model errors come from unclear reference material, not bad prompts. Fixing the source is cheaper, and more honest, than rewriting instructions blind.

Looking back

Scope

What I deliberately didn’t build matters as much as what I did

    With money and her name at stake, every send stays manual. Permanently.

  • Learning from corrections

    Her fix applies to her case. Claiming a self-improving system I hadn’t built would overstate it.

  • Accounts and settings

    The prototype’s job is proving the core loop.

  • Date dividers, jump-to-latest, tap-to-zoom

Reflection

What I’d do differently, and what I’d keep

Differently

  • Run the eval earlier. I polished screens on mock data while real model output was still unknown. My own build order said the eval should gate the UI.
  • Start with a sharper question. My first ideas were annoyances. The breakthrough was asking what was impossible before LLMs.
  • Recruit before designing. Desk research left two open risks, trust limits and willingness to pay, that only real people can answer.

Keep

  • The model reads and writes. Code counts. It caught real bugs before any user could see them.
  • Failure states with the same weight as the happy path.
  • Using the product myself. The biggest fix, pages to one thread, came from how it felt, not a checklist.

Questions worth asking

What people ask first

Is this legal advice?

No. It helps you complain and escalate, and never claims to be a lawyer.

Why not just use ChatGPT?

ChatGPT drafts. It doesn’t know your deadline, where to escalate, or follow up when the company goes quiet. Getbaq does all three, and still asks before sending anything.

What happens to my screenshots?

They build your case, nothing else. It never asks for your OTP, card or bank password, and strips an OTP if you paste one.

Does it ever send without me?

No. Every send needs your tap, with six seconds to cancel after.

What if it doesn’t know?

It says so, shows why, and asks.
