Designing an AI agent that fights for your refund

Ananya has ₹3,499 stuck with Flipkart and a bot that keeps saying “5 to 7 working days”. I designed an AI agent that finds the rule, counts the deadline and writes her complaint, and never sends a word without her.

Try the prototype

Short read · 9 minFull case study · 40 min

In 30 seconds

Challenge

Ananya has a legal right to her refund and a free government helpline that works, but doesn’t know the rule, the deadline or the channel. ChatGPT can draft her a letter. Nobody runs the fight.

Solution

An agent where she’s the approver. It reads her messy messages and screenshots, cites the rule, has code calculate the deadline, and drafts the complaint. Nothing is sent or escalated without her tap.

My role

Solo, about two weeks: problem selection, desk research, interaction and trust design, prompts and the evaluation plan, designed in code against a real model, Gemini.

Takeaways

The failure states are the design material. What the model is allowed to know is a design decision. And the evaluation, built but not yet run, is the result still missing.

Self-initiated, not client work. Not shipped to real users.

01 · Why an agent

A problem people couldn’t solve alone before LLMs

Ananya orders a mixer grinder on Flipkart for ₹3,499. The jar arrives rusted, her replacement is rejected because a photo “failed validation”, and three calls get her the same line: 5 to 7 working days. She has a legal right to the refund, and a free helpline that more than 1,000 companies answer. She doesn’t know either exists.

This is a job an LLM is actually suited to: reading messy evidence, matching it to the right rule, and writing a calm, specific letter, again and again, without tiring. ChatGPT can already draft the letter. But the letter is 10% of the job. Knowing the rule, the deadline and where to escalate, and following up when the company goes quiet, is the other 90%, and nobody does it for her.

I picked this over a higher-impact idea, a “trading brake” for young options traders, because that product fights its own user: 88.5% of under-30 traders lost money in FY26, and most kept trading anyway. A refund agent needs no behaviour change. She already wants the outcome.

Problem statement

She has the right to a refund and a free channel that works, but doesn’t know the rule, the channel or the deadline, and can’t afford the time to keep chasing. So the money stays with the company.

From desk research: complaints on Reddit and X, app reviews, consumer forums and government data. I couldn’t recruit interviews in time, and it’s labelled that way throughout.

02 · Show your work

The AI reads and writes. Code does every date and amount.

A confidently wrong deadline is worse than no agent: she’d wait for a date that has already passed. So before any screen, I decided what the AI is allowed to do at all. It handles judgment on messy text. Deadlines and money are tested code, and the model is never even given a day count, so it has nothing to miscalculate.

Then I made the split visible. Every step in the trace says who did it, the deadline says Calculated, and the rule cites the law it rests on, so she can check the work instead of taking it on trust.

03 · Approver, not autopilot

She approves every send, in her own name

Most agents hand over more control as trust grows. I chose not to. A wrong or rude letter damages her, not the AI, so on the Knight Institute’s autonomy levels, Ananya is an approver: the agent does the work, and nothing leaves without her.

But gating everything would make it useless. So I placed every action by how much is at stake and whether it can be undone, following Victor Yocco’s transparency framework, and let that decide the treatment. Only what’s high-stakes and can’t be undone waits for her tap:

Stakes ↑

Can be undone ← → Can’t be undone

Higher stakes · can be undone

Show it, and let her correct it

  • The facts it readrestated first: “You confirmed · Flipkart · ₹3,499 · 2 Aug”
  • The rule it pickedcited, with a confidence label and a reason
  • The deadlinecalculated by code, and labelled Calculated

Higher stakes · can’t be undone

Always her tap

  • Sending the complaintthe Approve card, her signature, six seconds to undo
  • Escalating to 1915prepared for her, and still her tap

Lower stakes · can be undone

Just do it, and show it

  • Reading her message or screenshota live trace of the real steps
  • Drafting the letterlabelled AI-drafted, and she can edit it

Lower stakes · can’t be undone

Do it, and tell her

  • Stripping an OTP she pastedremoved before the model sees it, and she’s told
  • 1

    It looks like an email. Familiar on purpose, so the novelty goes on the agent, not the letter. From shows her own name.

  • 2

    Amount and deadline first. The rule and how sure it is sit below, as backing.

  • 3

    One strong button. The only irreversible action looks unlike anything else; Edit and Not now are equally quiet.

  • 4

    Six seconds to undo, said right above the button she’s about to press.

The letter first had no name on it. Every one ended “Sent via Getbaq”, with nothing a grievance officer could match to her order. I’d treated “no accounts” as a feature, but a product that complains in someone’s name has to know who she is. Now, just above Send, it asks “Whose name goes on this letter?”: her name and the email or phone the company knows. Nothing more; it refuses Aadhaar, PAN and bank numbers if pasted.

04 · Designing for being wrong

Every failure ends in a next step, not a dead end

The core AI risk is a wrong rule she never thinks to question. So confidence is a plain label with a reason, never a bare number, and when it’s low, the Approve card never appears: she’s never asked to approve what the AI itself doubts.

Then I listed every way a case can go wrong, sorted by kind using Google PAIR’s error categories, and designed what she sees and what happens next for each:

05 · What I rejected

The options I tried or considered, and why they lost

Rejected

Why, and what replaced it

  • An autonomy dial: approve a few, then let it send

    A wrong or rude letter lands on her, not the AI. Approval stays, permanently.

  • Making her sort her own evidence (two versions)

    People won’t label a bot reply versus an order page versus a bank SMS. One box; sorting is the agent’s job.

  • A new page for every step

    It felt like being sent somewhere else each time. One thread; finished steps fold into receipts.

  • A pure chat

    The steps are decisions, so they’re cards she can act on, inside the conversation.

  • Asking who she is at the start

    It would feel like a sign-up form. The name is asked for just above Send, when it matters.

  • A confidence percentage

    A bare number invites false precision. A plain label with a reason, and no Approve when it’s low.

06 · Evals and limits

What’s tested, what’s built to be scored, and what isn’t proven

Caught before anyone used it. Testing the rules, the dates and the letters turned up five mistakes that would have cost people money: UPI got five days instead of RBI’s one working day; two airline refund clocks had been merged into one; deadlines shifted with the timezone near midnight; the model invented years when copying “2 Aug”; and UPI letters went to the shop instead of her bank. 210 automated tests now cover dates, money, states and safety.

8

legal rules, each read from the source and marked verified

30

real, sourced cases in the evaluation set

6

traps where the right answer is “no rule covers this”

3×

runs per case; it passes only if all three pass

The evaluation is built to catch the model inventing things: the case type, rule and amount are checked automatically, a person reads every draft for invented facts, and an invented rule fails outright. It hasn’t been run yet. The first attempt ran out of paid AI credit, and the free plan allows about 20 calls a day against the 90 it needs. The pass rate goes here when it exists, failures included.

Honest audit

  • Works today

    Gemini reads words and screenshots, picks the rule and writes the letter, all checked by code; Google sign-in carries her cases across devices

  • Designed, not built

    Sending from her own Gmail, which needs Google’s verification, and follow-ups that run while the app is closed

  • Not proven yet

    The evaluation score, and whether real people trust an agent to complain in their name

How I’ll know it works: money recovered, with a target of 3 of the first 5 real cases refunded. Behind it: days to refund, drafts sent without edits, a wrong-rule rate under 5% with zero invented rules, and no follow-up she has to write herself.

Looking back

Tradeoffs, and what I learned

Tradeoffs I made on purpose

What it costs, and why it’s worth it

  • A tap on every send

    Slower than an autopilot, but it’s her name and her money

  • No follow-ups while the app is closed

    Kept the prototype honest about what runs; she sees the next step when she opens the case

  • Simulated sending

    Real Gmail sending needs Google’s review; a broken flow would have been worse than a clear line

  • English only

    So the rule and tone checks can be tested in one language first; Hindi is the first thing I’d add

  • Desk research, not interviews

    Recruiting didn’t come through in time; trust limits and willingness to pay are still open

What I learned

Three things this taught me about designing AI products

  • 1

    The design material is the failure. Wrong, unsure, ignored and blocked are where trust is decided, so they deserve the same care as the happy path, and a real model to design against.

  • 2

    What the model is allowed to know is a design decision. Keeping day counts away from it made a whole class of wrong deadlines impossible, not just unlikely.

  • 3

    Trust comes from showing where things came from. Labelling every step AI, Calculated or Verified lets her check the work instead of taking it on faith.
