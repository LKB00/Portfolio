Designing an AI agent that fights for your refund

A customer has ₹3,499 stuck with Flipkart and a bot that keeps saying “5 to 7 working days”. I designed an AI agent that finds the rule, counts the deadline and writes their complaint, and never sends a word without them.

The story in a minute

The problem

A customer like this is owed an answer: the law says Flipkart must resolve their complaint within a month, and a free government helpline can push it. They don’t know the rule, the deadline or the channel, and they’re tired of chasing a bot. So the company keeps the money.

What I designed

ChatGPT can draft them a complaint. Nobody runs the whole fight: finds the rule, counts the days, follows up and escalates. Getbaq does. The AI reads and writes, tested code handles every date and amount, and nothing is sent without their tap.

Where it stands

A live prototype anyone can try, and five errors that would have cost people money, caught and fixed before anyone used it. Next: the scored evaluation, then five real cases.

How I worked

I designed in code, with a real AI model, not in static mock-ups. The product screens on this page are the working prototype itself, and Gemini reads what people send, so I designed against its actual answers and mistakes, not imagined ones. That’s how I found it inventing years in dates (chapter 2). An AI coding assistant wrote much of the code; the research, decisions, prompts and evaluation are mine.

The problem

Choose a problem where AI is the product, not a feature

Which problem is worth an AI agent, who has it, and when is the moment to step in?

The starting question

I didn’t start with refunds. I started with a question.

Most AI features I’d seen were a chatbot added to a product that already worked. I wanted the reverse: a problem that only became practical to solve with LLMs, so that the AI is the product, not decoration. So I started with a question instead of a solution.

What can a young Indian do now that only became practical with LLMs?

Three rounds of ideas

The first two rounds failed my own filter

I ran three rounds of ideas against those tests. The first two taught me what the filter was really for: telling a real loss apart from an annoyance.

Round 1

Annoyances, not problems

Scam-checking, a screenshot organiser, a money-leak tracker. Nobody loses real money to a messy camera roll, and a big player could ship any of these in a week.

Failed the filter

Round 2

Scale, but not sharp

Job readiness: huge, crowded, vague. Scam protection: thousands of crores lost every year, but very hard to distribute.

Too broad

Round 3

A better question

Not “what’s painful”, but “what only became practical with LLMs”: an advocate for people who can’t afford one, who knows the rules and never tires of following up.

Research

I couldn’t recruit in time, so I changed the method, not the questions

I planned 5–8 interviews with people holding a stuck refund. Recruiting didn’t come through in time, so I answered the same questions from public evidence: Reddit and X complaints, app reviews, consumer forums, news and government data. It’s labelled desk research everywhere, never primary research, and every finding below carries a confidence label, so strong evidence and educated guesses never look the same.

A representative user

Built from desk research, not interviews

  • First or second job. Pays with UPI, India’s instant phone-to-bank payments, not a card.

  • Orders on Flipkart, Meesho or Myntra about once a month.

  • Has ₹500–₹20,000 stuck: too small for a lawyer, big enough to hurt.

  • Already tried the bot once and got “5 to 7 working days.”

  • Doesn’t know the 1915 helpline exists.

Picture them in August. The mixer grinder they ordered for ₹3,499 arrives with a rusted jar. They ask for a replacement, and it’s rejected because a photo “failed validation”. They call three times and get nowhere. That Flipkart case runs through the rest of this story, and it’s the sample the prototype opens with.

The lowest point isn’t the loss. It’s stage 5, when there’s no next step. That’s the moment it’s built for.

The focus moment

They’ve just been told “5–7 working days”, again

They’re angry, on their phone, deciding whether to chase again or give up. It’s in almost every source, and it’s when an advocate is worth most. Earlier, they still trust the company. Later, they’ve stopped caring.

Problem statement

The law is on their side and a free channel works, but they don’t know the rule, the channel or the deadline, and can’t afford the time to keep chasing. So the money stays with the company.

It puts the cause on missing knowledge and missing time, not on them. An AI agent can supply both.

ChatGPT is the real default

People already paste “write a strong complaint” into it. But the letter is the easy part. Knowing the rule, where to send it, when to escalate, and following up is the real work.

DoNotPay is the cautionary tale

It called itself a “robot lawyer” and paid $193,000 to settle an FTC case for overpromising. Getbaq never claims to be a lawyer, on any screen.

Companies already have AI

Tools like Intercom Fin auto-resolve support tickets. The consumer side has almost nothing that runs the whole fight.

The risk, stated openly

The helpline could add its own AI; Voxya could add an LLM. The defence is owning the whole fight, from rule to escalation to follow-up, where each rival does one step.

Three assumptions I changed1915 moved from last resort to first escalation, and two more.

People give up after 2–3 weeks

No give-up trigger

No source supports a fixed timeline. So the product removes follow-up effort from day one.

1915 is a last resort

1915 moved to the front

It’s fast, and 1,000+ companies already respond to it. It became the early, primary escalation.

I know the trust limits

Designed conservatively

There’s no India evidence on fees or trust. So: free to start, approval on every action, and both stated as open risks.

The agent had to know the rules, so I read them first, from the primary sources rather than summaries: the E-Commerce Rules 2020; the circular from RBI, India’s central bank, on failed payments; the RBI Ombudsman, its free complaint service; the refund rules of DGCA, the aviation regulator; and the helpline’s programme that connects it to companies. And I collected 30 real cases, anonymised and sourced, that later became the eval set: 12 e-commerce, 7 travel, 4 UPI, 4 ed-tech or subscription, and 3 that are out of scope or fit no category. Six are traps with no matching rule.

From problem to principles

Five principles that settled every design argument

Before drawing a screen, I turned the research into five principles. Whenever two options were both reasonable, these decided between them, and they come back in every chapter that follows.

  • 1

    Effort down, not process up. If a screen adds work, cut it.

  • 2

    Nothing leaves without a tap. Every send is previewed and approved.

  • 3

    Show the rule, the deadline and the doubt. Cite sources. Say when unsure.

  • 4

    Factual, never angry. Short, calm complaints are easier to act on.

  • 5

    Honest about limits. It helps you complain. It is not a lawyer.

Where AI stops

The model reads and writes. Code counts.

Which parts need AI judgment, which need exact calculation, and how would I know the AI is right?

The capability map

AI for judgment, code for anything exact, and a list it never touches

An agent that’s confidently wrong about a deadline is worse than no agent: the user would wait for a date that has already passed. So the first design decision here wasn’t a screen. It was deciding which parts of the job the AI is allowed to do at all.

Deadlines and money must be exact, so they’re tested code. The AI is only needed for judgment on messy text: their message, a screenshot, a company’s reply. The “never” list came straight from the trust research.

AI

AI reads.

  • Works out what kind of evidence a screenshot is
  • Picks the one rule that applies
  • Drafts a short, factual complaint
  • Judges whether a reply actually resolves it
  • Says how sure it is, and why

Code

Code counts.

  • Deadline maths: 48 hours, 30 days, ₹100 a day
  • Which facts are required, and asking for them
  • The escalation ladder and its timers
  • Case status and the evidence record
  • Routing: company, 1915 or bank

You

You decide.

  • Confirms the facts it read
  • Edits the draft, or says no
  • Signs and sends, in their own name
  • Approves every escalation
  • Closes the case if it resolved another way
  1. Interpretation
  2. Verification
  3. Action

AI never does

  • Sends anything without their tap
  • Gives legal advice or predicts an outcome
  • Asks for an OTP, card or bank details
  • Invents a rule or a fact
  • Writes in an angry tone

And the user can see the split: every step is labelled with who did it, and the deadline says Calculated, not AI. Every rule card cites the law it rests on, the way Perplexity puts its sources first, so they can check it instead of taking it on trust.

The legal line

In India, practising law is reserved for enrolled advocates. So Getbaq helps them complain and escalate, never advises, and every action screen says so.

Guardrails

The model’s output is never trusted directly

Drawing the line isn’t enough, because the model will still sometimes misbehave. So nothing it writes reaches them until code has checked it, and each check ends in something they can see, never a silent failure:

If the model…

Code…

  • Puts an amount or date in the letter they didn’t confirm

    Drops the letter and uses the template, and says so

  • Asks for the full amount back on an overcharge, instead of just the difference

    Refuses the letter: only the confirmed difference may appear

  • Cites a rule that isn’t on the verified sheet

    Routes to Not sure. It can never reach Approve.

  • Writes more than 150 words

    Asks once to shorten, then drops the draft

  • Says “sue”, “legal action”, “lawyer”, threatens, or uses “!”

    Asks once to rewrite, then drops it

  • Is unsure

    Never shows the Approve button

  • Receives an OTP-like code

    Strips it before the model sees it, and tells them

  • Tries to send without a tap

    Can’t: only a real tap records a send

Bugs I caught

Five mistakes that would have cost people money, caught at the source, and one known gap

Testing the rules, the dates and the letters, before anyone used the product, turned up five mistakes. Each would have cost someone money or days they didn’t have, and none of them would have shown up in a mock-up:

    Every failed payment got 5 days.

    Risk

    Actual

The full prompt and its guardrails are built and covered by tests, and the live prototype uses them: Gemini reads what they send, picks the rule, writes the letter and reads the company’s reply. The user’s control

The user approves everything, in their own name

How much should an agent do on its own when it’s their money and their name?

The lifecycle

It proposes, they approve, it acts, it reports back

An agent that acts on its own is the whole appeal, and the whole risk. A wrong or rude letter goes out under the user’s name, to a company that is holding their money. So every action has the same four beats, whoever set it off. Escalation isn’t exempt: even after a company has ignored them for weeks, the 1915 complaint still needs their tap.

It says back what it understood before it acts. Before any rule is found or letter written, the user sees “You confirmed · Flipkart · ₹3,499 · 2 Aug” and can fix a wrong fact there, not in a finished letter. It’s the pattern known as prompt reflection: the AI restates the request before doing anything with it.

Agentic decisions

No autonomy dial, on purpose

A common pattern is to hand over more control as trust grows: approve the first few, then let it run. I chose not to. Here a wrong or rude message damages them, not the AI, so growing trust never removes the need to approve. In the language of the Knight Institute’s autonomy levels, The user is an approver: the agent does the work, and nothing leaves without them.

Not every action needs the same gate, though. I placed each one by how much is at stake and whether it can be undone, following Victor Yocco’s transparency framework, and the treatment follows from where it lands. Only what’s both high-stakes and irreversible waits for their tap:

OTPs, PINs and passwords never reach the model.

They’re stripped from the message before the model reads it, whatever the stakes. This is a rule, so it sits outside the matrix.

Every step shown is real work that just finished. A fake step is the same violation as a fake rule.

The craft

Smaller decisions that make it feel like one product

The two changes that mattered most, then the smaller ones, each with the reason it changed.

Iteration

From pages to one thread

Using my own prototype, every step felt like being sent to a new page. I kept the logic the same and changed only how it looks: each step adds to one conversation, and finished steps fold into one-line receipts. It reads like a conversation but isn’t only chat: the steps are cards they can act on. As Perplexity’s head of design puts it, not everything should be a chat.

This did more for it feeling like an agent than any colour, font or animation.

Iteration

One box that takes anything

People won’t sort a bot reply from an order page from a bank SMS. The first two versions made them sort; the third made it the agent’s job.

The user shouldn’t have to understand the agent’s input model before they can use it.

One box, but not one process. Text is scrubbed for OTPs, then read. A screenshot is read with vision, which is slower, and the trace says so.

Read once, ask onceEvery missing fact asked for in one card.

Missing info used to ask one question per turn: company, then date, each a full round trip for something the agent knew it needed from the start. Now code decides what the case type requires and asks for every gap in one card. Each question says why it’s needed and where to find it, offers likely answers, and moves on by itself.

Two places, one thread per case, and three loopsThree loops, run by code: escalation, correction and missing information.

A bug the motion found

The first version of the phone sheet made the conversation behind it jump 239 pixels as it opened. Giving the sheet keyboard focus while it was still below the screen made the browser scroll to show it. The fix was one option (focus without scrolling), plus a container that can’t scroll at all, so it can’t come back.

Their own words outgrew their type sizeSet for a short tap, then the reply started sending full sentences.

Their own message was set in the same big serif as the landing headline and the case’s final amount: right for a tap like “Zoomcar” or “yes”, too loud once the “zoom car” fix (chapter 4) meant it carried full sentences like “CarDekho charged me more than they should have”, wrapping two lines above the agent’s own 14px reply. Cut from 32px to 26px for the first line and 24px to 19px for every one after, keeping the same proportion between them.

Looking back

What I learned

Three things this taught me about designing AI products

  • 1

    The design material is the failure. Wrong, unsure, ignored and blocked are where trust is decided, so they deserve the same care as the happy path, and a real model to design against.

  • 2

    What the model is allowed to know is a design decision. Keeping day counts away from it made a whole class of wrong deadlines impossible, not just unlikely.

  • 3

    Trust comes from showing where things came from. Labelling every step AI, Calculated or Verified lets them check the work instead of taking it on faith.

Reflection

What I’d do differently, and what I’d keep

Differently

  • Run the eval earlier. I designed against the model’s real answers one case at a time, but polished screens before its answers were scored across all 30. The score should come before the polish.

  • Recruit before designing. Trust limits and willingness to pay are still open. Only real people can close them.

  • Write the marketing from the code. The landing page promised more than the prototype did. The audit caught it; it shouldn’t have had to.

  • Question “no accounts” earlier. A complaint needs a name on it (chapter 3).

Keep

  • The model reads and writes. Code counts.

  • Failure states with the same weight as the happy path.

  • Using the product myself. The biggest fix, one thread, came from how it felt.

How I’ll know it works

Proposed success criteria. Not measured yet.

Money recovered

The only number the user cares about. Target: 3 of the first 5 real cases refunded. Not measured yet.

Days to refund

Target: inside the rule’s own deadline, beating the manual journey.

Drafts sent unedited

Draft quality, and trust. Target: most sent with no edits, or small ones.

Wrong-rule rate

Measures invented law directly. Target: under 5% on the eval, and zero invented rules.

Follow-ups by the agent

Effort removed. Target: they never write a follow-up themselves.

The targets are hypotheses to test, not results. The riskiest assumptions, ranked: people let an agent send in their name; companies answer rule-backed complaints faster; the helpline accepts agent-prepared ones; the AI picks the right rule; and people will pay, which this project didn’t test.

What people ask firstLegal advice, ChatGPT, screenshots, and five more.
Is this legal advice?

No. It helps you complain and escalate, and never claims to be a lawyer.

Why not just use ChatGPT?

ChatGPT drafts. It doesn’t know your deadline, where to escalate, or follow up when the company goes quiet. Getbaq does all three, and still asks before sending anything.

What happens to my screenshots?

They’re read to build your case, and Getbaq doesn’t keep them: the case only notes that you sent one. It never asks for your OTP, card or bank password, and strips an OTP if you paste one.

Does it ever send without me?

No. Every send needs your tap, and you get six seconds to cancel before it goes. In this prototype, nothing is actually sent.

Why do I sign the letter?

Because the complaint is from you, not from Getbaq. Companies match it to your account by your name and the email or phone you use with them.

What if it doesn’t know?

It says so, shows why, and asks.
