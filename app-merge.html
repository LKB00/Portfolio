The short version

Rupeezy had two apps — a trading app carrying 97% of company revenue, and a separate mutual fund app.

A SEBI regulation cut trading revenue by roughly 75%, and merging stopped being optional. The obvious answer was a mode switch between products, so that’s what shipped first.

I didn’t believe in it. I shipped it anyway, argued it was wrong for a broker, and designed the model that replaced it — navigation split into two axes, one for the journey and one for the product. It’s live in Rupeezy today across stocks, F&O and commodities.

01 — Context

Two apps

A stockbroker founded in 2003, digital since 2017. Around 125 people.

RUPEEZY TRADE

Stocks and F&O. 97% of company revenue. I owned this product end to end.

RUPEEZY INVEST

Mutual funds. Much smaller.

A user who held both had two logins and two partial views of their own money — the mutual fund app couldn’t show their equity holdings, and the trading app couldn’t show their funds. To answer “how am I doing?”, they had to open both and add it up themselves. Rupeezy had a mutual fund product; there was just no way to sell it from inside the trading app, so the most valuable slot on that home screen went to a banner pushing users out to download it separately.

The split cost us three things. A mutual fund investor couldn’t buy a stock and a trader couldn’t start an SIP, so there was no cross-sell in either direction. Every compliance change and every campaign had to be built twice. And 97% of revenue sitting in one product line isn’t concentration — it’s a single point of failure. Everyone knew this. Merging had been discussed for a long time. Nothing forced it.

Rupeezy Trade’s home screen before the merge, promoting Rupeezy’s own mutual fund app — a separate download for the other half of your portfolio. The banner’s position wasn’t fixed; it moved up or down depending on what else needed space. Mutual funds competed for slots on the trading app’s home screen like an unrelated product. After the merge, they stopped being a banner and became a tab.

That banner is the entire business problem, sitting in production, in our own app.

02 — The forcing function

Then something forced it

October 2024SEBI announced new derivatives rules: larger contract sizes, higher premiums, designed to push small retail traders out of F&O. Industry commentary expected brokers to lose around 60% of revenue.

We were hit harder than the industry estimate because our business was concentrated in exactly the segment the regulation targeted.

Mutual funds were no longer additional revenue. They were the only other business we had.

03 — Phase 1

The version I shipped and didn’t believe in

The first meeting decided the direction — fold Invest into Trade, because Trade had the users and the revenue. The reference was Swiggy switching between food and Instamart: a switch that changes the entire app context. The reasoning was speed, and it was correct.

Where the switch lived was my call. I ran three iterations.

Top navigationFloating buttonBottom navigation

Top navigation

rejected

The top bar already carries indices, search and notifications. Adding a switch meant redesigning screens we weren’t otherwise touching.

Floating button on Home

rejected

It can only live on one screen. A user three levels into a stock page would have to navigate all the way out to change products.

Bottom navigation

chosen

Present on every screen, so the switch is always one tap away wherever the user already is. It disturbed the existing layout least.

Phase 1 shipped — 15 January 2025

I didn’t believe in it.

Not the execution — that was fine, and it was fast. I didn’t believe in the model underneath it. Refusing would have been precious, so I shipped it and kept building the version I thought was right.

04 — The argument

Why a switch is wrong for a broker

The mode-switch pattern was the obvious answer. It was also the wrong one. A mode switch works when the two modes are genuinely different jobs.

Ordering dinner and buying groceries are different jobs — different intent, different session. Stocks and mutual funds are not. They’re two ways of doing the same thing: putting money somewhere and watching what it does.

The portfolio

If I want to check my portfolio, I want my portfolio — my stocks and my funds are both my money. Under a switch, the smallest possible movement between two views of the same thing changes the entire application.

The axis

A switch makes product the primary axis and task the secondary one. For a broker, it’s the wrong way round.

Scale

A switch is binary, and commodities were already being discussed. Every product after the second one is either a new mode, or a switch that quietly turns into a menu.

For a broker, that’s backwards.

05 — The model

The journey is invariant. The product is variable.

Not what makes these products different — what stays the same across all of them.

Bottom navigation — the journey

Home · Explore · Tools · Orders · Portfolio

Top tabs — the product

Stocks · F&O · Mutual Funds · Next

Stocks, funds, F&O, commodities, anything the business adds in five years — that sequence never changes. Only the asset class does.

Whichever axis you’re on stays fixed while the other varies.

One structure, read two ways, depending on what you came to do.

06 — Home

Home is the exception

Every other screen is scoped to one product. Home has no product tabs at all, because it’s where cross-sell has to happen.

One number

Overall portfolio at the top: everything the user holds, across every product, totalled.

Equal weight

IPO Live and NFO Live sit side by side. New stock listings and new fund offers get the same billing.

First-timer language

Fund categories a beginner recognises — hybrid, ELSS, invest with ₹100 — not industry classifications.

This was the first time a Rupeezy user could see everything they owned in one number.

A trader opening this screen sees mutual funds sitting next to the stocks they came for. Not as an ad. As part of the same product.

The banner is gone, because the product it pointed to is now on this screen.

07 — Detail

Four decisions that follow from the model.

Portfolio aggregates at the top, separates in detail.

Stock holdings and fund holdings are different objects: quantity and day change versus units and NAV. They combine only on Home, as totals.

The top bar scrolls. The bottom bar doesn’t.

Three tabs fit today; when commodities arrive the bar extends instead of breaking. The axis that grows is the one that can grow.

Tools stayed shared.

Basket orders, alerts, global markets, strategies, and MF Lab for comparing funds. It was never stock-only, so it never needed tabs.

Tabs persist across sessions.

Someone who only holds mutual funds isn’t asked to re-select mutual funds every time. First-time users default to Stocks, because most users are traders.

A note on the default

The default-tab call is the one I’m least comfortable with. Defaulting to Stocks is the majority answer, and it’s slightly wrong for exactly the users the merge was meant to attract.

08 — Language

Ten slots became five.

Position

F&O vocabulary. You hold a position in derivatives, and you close it. A mutual fund investor doesn’t hold positions — they hold investments, for years.

Watchlist

A trader’s habit. You watch a stock because you’re waiting on a price. Fund investors don’t watch and wait; they browse categories and compare.

Navigation written for traders would have quietly told every mutual fund investor this app wasn’t built for them.

Structure decides language. I didn’t set out to rename the navigation — the architecture made it necessary.

09 — Migration

Architecture solves the product. It doesn’t move anyone.

KYC was already unified across both apps, so a user verified in Invest was already verified in Trade. Accounts linked automatically — no re-verification, no documents, no starting over.

In a regulated product, that’s the difference between a migration and an abandonment.

01
Dismissible

A banner in Rupeezy Invest announcing the move. Information, and the user keeps control of their screen.

02
Non-dismissible

The same banner, no longer closable. Still the old app, but the move is no longer optional.

03
Removed

The app leaves the stores. At this point the banner isn’t in the app; it is the app.

We didn’t want to trap anyone at the start. We did need everyone moved by the end.

10 — Buy-in

Getting it approved

I explained the two-axis model in a meeting. It didn’t land.

Talking about navigation models in the abstract rarely does — the difference between “a switch” and “two axes” is hard to hear and easy to see.

So over a weekend I built it. Real screens, clickable, showing what it feels like to move between portfolio views without the app changing underneath you.

The initial preference was still the simpler option — it was already built and already shipped. I walked through why mode-switching is wrong for a broker, why two axes scale as products get added, and why this protects existing fund investors instead of absorbing them.

If I can’t convince people with words, the problem is usually the format, not the argument.

11 — Outcome

What shipped

The two-axis navigation is live in Rupeezy today, across stocks, F&O and commodities.

Mutual funds are still separate.

I left in August 2025 while it was in development, so I don’t know why — sequencing, compliance, or a decision the team made after I’d gone.

The honest outcome

The architecture I argued for is in production and carrying the products it was built to scale across. The merge it was designed to enable hasn’t fully happened.

What I’d do differently

No user testing

I never spoke to a user about this one. The decisions came from the business, the data and my own reasoning.

No measurement plan

Nobody agreed what this should move before we shipped, so I can’t tell you whether cross-sell improved.

Two apps, one architecture, one unfinished merge.

Happy to go into what I’d do differently, or the parts I still think about.
