The short version

Rupeezy had two apps. A trading app carrying 97% of company revenue, and a separate mutual fund app.

A SEBI regulation cut trading revenue by roughly 75%, and merging stopped being optional. The obvious answer was a mode switch between products, so that’s what shipped first.

A switch holds two products, and commodities were already coming. I designed the model that replaced it. Navigation split into two axes, one for the journey and one for the product. The team agreed it was right, and it waited behind the regulation work until after I’d gone. It’s live in Rupeezy today across stocks, F&O and commodities.

01 · Where this started

Two apps, one customer, and a banner pointing at the other one

A stockbroker founded in 2003, digital since 2017, around 125 people, selling two products through two separate apps.

RUPEEZY TRADE

Stocks and F&O. 97% of company revenue. I owned this product end to end.

RUPEEZY INVEST

Mutual funds. Much smaller.

Neither app could show a complete portfolio. To answer “how am I doing”, a user opened both and reconciled the two totals themselves.

That banner is the entire business problem, sitting in production, in our own app.

Before the problem, the people it belonged to.

02 · Who was involved

One designer, one PM, seven engineers, and a room that met once

The direction was set in a single kickoff that covered scope, timeline and who owned what. I was the only designer in it.

The room

Managing Director, two VP Products, my design manager, and me. The people who could say yes, and the one who would draw it.

The team

One PM, seven engineers, and me as sole designer, reporting to a design manager who reviewed but didn’t design.

The user

A retail trader who also held mutual funds. Two logins, two partial views of one portfolio.

How I learned what I knew

Play Store reviews, where users were already asking why they needed two apps when every other broker had one. In-app feedback and Firebase for how people moved between the two. No interviews.

I never spoke to a user directly. Everything above is what I had instead.

That’s who it was for. Here’s what was wrong.

03 · What was broken

97% of revenue in one product, and a regulation coming for it

The split cost three things every day. Then a rule made it existential.

No cross-sell

A fund investor couldn’t buy a stock, a trader couldn’t start an SIP, and anything we built next could only reach half our own customers.

Built twice

Two developers per app, four in total, shipping every compliance change and every campaign twice.

Single point of failure

97% of revenue in one product line isn’t concentration. It’s exposure.

Users had noticed too. Reviews put it plainly. Every other broker has one app, so why do I need two?

Diversifying into mutual funds had been the plan for a long time. It was never urgent enough to start.

Then a regulation set the date.

October 2024SEBI announced new derivatives rules. Larger contracts and higher premiums, designed to push small retail traders out of F&O.
November 2024Design work began.
15 January 2025Phase 1 shipped.

Mutual funds were no longer additional revenue. They were the only other business we had.

That’s the problem. What we wanted from fixing it is next.

04 · What we were after

The goal was never a merge

It was to stop being a business that only made money when people traded.

For the business

Diversify away from F&O. Sell to both halves of our own customer base. Build compliance and campaigns once, not twice.

For the user

One place where all your money lives. One number that says how you’re doing, without opening a second app.

This looked like a navigation problem. It was a business model change.

The plan had two phases, and the first one had to ship fast.

05 · How I went at it

I shipped the fast version first

Phase one: get Invest inside Trade with a mode switch between them, the pattern food-delivery apps use to move between food and groceries. Phase two: refine once it was live. I delivered phase one and kept working.

Where the switch lived was my call. I ran three iterations.

rejected

Top navigation

The top bar already carries indices, search and notifications. Adding a switch meant redesigning screens we weren’t otherwise touching.

rejected

Floating button on Home

It can only live on one screen. A user three levels into a stock page would have to navigate all the way out to change products.

chosen

Bottom navigation

Present on every screen, so the switch is always one tap away wherever the user already is. It disturbed the existing layout least.

Phase 1 shipped, 15 January 2025

But a switch holds two products. Commodities were already being discussed.

The switch worked. I could already see where it wouldn’t.

06 · What got in the way

Three things stood in the way

The model I wanted didn’t exist yet. It wasn’t in scope. And once it did exist, it had to wait for capacity.

One: a switch doesn’t scale

Stocks and mutual funds aren’t two different jobs. They’re two ways of doing the same one, which is putting money somewhere and watching what it does.

Two directions run through the app: what you own, and what you’re doing. Call them axes.

Axis

A switch fixes one axis permanently. In practice, whichever axis you’re on should hold still while the other moves.

Scale

A switch is binary. Every product after the second is a new mode, or a menu doing a switch’s job.

Two: explaining it

I explained the two-axis model out loud. The difference between “a switch” and “two axes” is hard to hear and easy to see.

If I can’t convince people with words, the problem is usually the format, not the argument.

Three: getting it into the roadmap

I showed the two-axis screens to the design team first, covering what I’d built, why, and where the switch would break. Then to a VP Product and a Director. Everyone saw it. Capacity was the constraint. The regulation had added work to every team’s pipeline, the switch was live and working, and this meant real engineering effort on top.

So it was agreed, and deferred. Phase two, when there was room.

The model was agreed and waiting. Here’s what it became.

07 · What I built

Ten navigation slots became five

One bottom bar for the journey, one product bar on top, and a migration that didn’t trap anyone.

Position

F&O vocabulary. You hold a position and you close it. A fund investor holds investments, for years.

Watchlist

A trader’s habit. You watch a stock because you’re waiting on a price. Fund investors browse and compare.

Home is the exception

With tabs, a trader on Home only ever sees stocks. The merge exists so they see funds too.

One number

Everything you hold, across every product.

Equal weight

IPO and NFO side by side, so neither product is the guest.

First-timer language

Hybrid, ELSS, invest with ₹100. Words a beginner recognises.

The call
Whether Home follows the same product-tab pattern as every other screen.
Considered
Give Home the same product bar. Consistent with Explore, Orders and Portfolio, and the user always knows which product they’re in.
Chose
No tabs on Home. Every product visible at once.
Cost
One screen breaks the pattern, and Home is the only place where scope isn’t explicit.

The product bar scrolls, reusing the tab pattern already used for pending and executed orders. The bottom bar doesn’t move.

The call
Which product tab a first-time user lands on.
Considered
Always default to Stocks. Simple, and right for most users.
Chose
Use the intent captured in KYC. Someone who said they came to invest lands on Mutual Funds. Stocks only when intent is unknown.
Cost
The fallback still favours traders, so a fund investor who skipped the question starts in the wrong place.

Moving people without shocking them

KYC was already unified, so accounts linked automatically. No re-verification, no documents.

The app leaves the stores. At this point the banner isn’t in the app; it is the app.

A hard cutover would have been faster. In a money app, an app that suddenly stops working reads as something broken, not as an upgrade.

It shipped in stages. What’s live today is next.

08 · What happened next

The architecture shipped. The merge is still going.

Built after I left, to the model that was agreed and deferred.

The two-axis navigation is live in Rupeezy today across stocks, F&O and commodities. Commodities arrived after I left. The model was deferred for capacity, then built when there was room.

It now holds a product I never designed for.

Mutual funds hadn’t been folded in when I left in August 2025. I don’t know what’s happened since.

What I’d do differently

No user testing

I never spoke to a user about this one. The decisions came from the business, the data and my own reasoning.

No measurement plan

We didn’t agree what this should move before we shipped, so I can’t tell you whether cross-sell improved.
