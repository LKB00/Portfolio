Unifying two investing apps without disrupting familiar workflows

Why merge?

One business. Two investment journeys.

Rupeezy Trade served stock and F&O investors. Rupeezy Invest, the Investeezy app, served mutual-fund investors. Each had its own product experience and navigation.

Rupeezy Trade · stocks and F&O

For active traders. Funds appear only as a banner for the other app.

Rupeezy Invest · mutual funds

For fund investors, with its own five tabs.

The business also wanted customers to reach both products easily, and one place to build for.

Two apps meant building features twice, making compliance changes twice and running marketing twice. The goal was one product experience with less duplicated work.

Business context. Around 97% of company revenue came from Trade at the time. Company-reported, not a design outcome.

Frame the problem

One app needed to stay familiar and still grow.

The challenge was to bring two product experiences into one app without making familiar tasks harder to find. We also had six weeks for the first release, and wanted to avoid major changes to the existing code.

Options & Version 1 · shipped

The practical first step was a switch.

The first direction came from senior stakeholders. With six weeks and a preference for minimal code change, the team chose a simple switch between Trade and Invest rather than rebuilding both experiences. I designed the agreed switch-based solution, a pragmatic way to bring both products into one app while keeping the first release focused.

How the directions compared

The limit of the switch

A switch works for two. What happens if there are more?

While designing Version 1, I began questioning how the switch would behave if Rupeezy grew beyond Trade and Invest. Each new product could become another mode to switch into. I saw an opportunity to separate product selection from the tasks people came to complete.

Version 1 · the switch

Every new product becomes another mode.

The button flips between Trade and Invest. A third product would sit behind the same button, with its own set of tabs.

Concept · two layers

Every new product becomes one more tab.

The product row already had a slot marked New. A product adds a tab here; the task bar below stays the same.

Design hypothesis. A potential scalability concern, not a measured failure or a confirmed roadmap.

Two layers, one model · concept

Separate the product from the task.

I proposed a two-layer navigation. The first layer chooses a product: Stocks, F&O or Funds. The second keeps Home, Explore, Tools, Orders and Portfolio in the same places. Product content changes; the task navigation doesn’t.

Prototype & open questions

The idea was hard to picture. I made it tangible.

Explaining the alternative verbally didn’t land. I built a clickable prototype to explore the interaction, then presented static screens to the design team and stakeholders. They saw value and supported further exploration, but implementation was deferred for engineering bandwidth and priorities.

From my original screen set; not tested with users.

Which product should open first?

Most users were traders, so Stocks was a familiar default. But the business wanted funds to grow, and always opening on Stocks could keep Funds out of sight. I had no evidence for either, so I’d test the trade-off rather than assume an answer.

Migration

A new app also needs a clear way in.

The merge affected existing Invest users too. Shared KYC meant nobody verified again. Banners announced the move, but some users weren’t sure which app they were in or which to use, so we improved the messaging before widening the rollout. The old app was later removed from the stores.

What I would validate next

The project showed the difference between a practical first release and a longer-term architecture question. Next, I’d validate the two-layer model with traders and fund investors, and agree success measures before implementation.

  1. Hypothesis

    People can tell the product they’re in apart from the common tasks, and predict where a task lives.

  2. Tree test

    Can people find tasks in the proposed product and task structure?

  3. Task-based usability test

    Representative tasks, such as finding an order or reviewing a portfolio, with Stocks or Funds opening first. Watch navigation choices and confusion.

  4. Evaluate

    Task completion, first-click accuracy, wrong turns and backtracking, and where people got confused. Traders must not be slowed down to promote Funds.

  5. Iterate

    Revise the hierarchy and labels based on what we observe.

Proposed plan, not completed research. None of this was run.

Merging two products is an information-architecture problem.

The first release brought Trade and Invest together. Designing it also exposed how navigation should work when one app serves several investment products. My contribution wasn’t shipping the alternative: it was spotting the concern, making the concept tangible, and keeping it distinct from what the team actually delivered.
